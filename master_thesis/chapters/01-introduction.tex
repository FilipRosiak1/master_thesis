The genotype is not a fixed-length vector in which every coordinate has an independent and stable meaning. Consequently, the same local symbol or fragment may have a different effect depending on its position and surrounding genotype context.

This context dependence limits the effectiveness of naive edits and makes the definition of useful neighborhoods non-trivial.

Evolutionary design studies the automatic generation and improvement of arteficts by means of evolutionary computation. Instead of manually specifying a complete design, a human designer defines a representation, variation operators, and a fitness criterion, while the search process explores possible arteficts. Such arteficts may include engineering structures, robot morphologies, artificial organisms, programs, or other objects whose quality can be assessed by an objective function or by simulation. This makes evolutionary design a broad research area rather than a technique tied to one particular environment or representation \cite{bentley1999introduction}.

A central issue in evolutionary design is the choice of representation. The representation determines which candidate designs can be expressed, how variation changes them, and how genotype-level modifications are translated into phenotype-level effects. Previous work distinguishes many representation families, including direct and indirect encodings, fixed-length and variable-length genotypes, linear and non-linear structures, grammatical encodings, developmental encodings, graph-based representations, and generative representations \cite{bentley1999introduction,kicinger2005evolutionary}. These alternatives differ not only in expressiveness, but also in the structure of the search space that an optimization algorithm has to explore.

The problem considered in this thesis belongs to the class of optimization problems in which candidate solutions are represented by structured, variable-length, non-positional genotypes. In such representations, the meaning of a fragment is not determined only by its local textual form or by a fixed coordinate. Instead, its effect may depend on syntax, nesting and neighbouring fragments.
% , and the genotype-to-phenotype mapping. 
A local modification that is meaningful in one context may be invalid, neutral, or harmful in another. This context dependence makes the definition of useful variation operators and neighbourhoods non-trivial.

The thesis compares two representation-aware approaches to this problem. Compatible Substitutions Optimization (CoSO) operates directly in the discrete genotype space and attempts to propose substitutions between fragments that are compatible with their surrounding context. Latent Space Optimization (LSO) follows a different strategy: it learns a continuous representation of genotypes and performs search in that learned space before decoding candidates back to the original representation. CoSO can therefore be interpreted as an explicit, context-aware discrete approach, whereas LSO is an implicit, learned, continuous-representation approach.

The empirical part of the thesis instantiates this general problem using Framsticks \fone{} genotypes as a case study. \fone{} genotypes are variable-length symbolic descriptions with a branching, tree-like interpretation, and candidate solutions are evaluated in Framsticks with respect to the \vertpos{} criterion. Framsticks is therefore used as a concrete experimental environment for comparing CoSO, LSO, and a standard evolutionary baseline, rather than as the only possible domain to which the studied approaches apply.

Evolutionary design is motivated by the possibility of exploring large and complex design spaces automatically. In many domains, useful artifacts are difficult to design by explicitly specifying every component and relation in advance. Evolutionary methods make it possible to search over alternative structures, morphologies, or construction rules, provided that candidate artifacts can be represented, varied, and evaluated. This is especially useful when the quality of a design emerges only after simulation or phenotype construction.

The motivation for this thesis comes from the observation that representation strongly influences what an evolutionary search can discover. A simple fixed-length vector can be convenient for optimization, but it may be poorly suited for designs whose size, topology, or hierarchy should change during search. More expressive representations, including developmental and generative encodings, can support modularity, reuse, hierarchy, and structural regularity, but they also make variation more difficult to control \cite{bentley1999three,hornby2003generative,kicinger2005evolutionary}. Therefore, improving search in evolutionary design often requires methods that are aware of the representation rather than operators that treat all positions or symbols independently.

Variable-length structured representations highlight this challenge particularly clearly. Candidate solutions are not simple collections of independent parameters. They may contain fragments whose interpretation depends on grammar, position in a tree, branch structure, or the construction process that maps genotype to phenotype. Random local edits may explore the space widely, but they can also destroy syntactic validity or disrupt useful substructures. This creates practical motivation to study optimization methods that preserve useful fragments and propose modifications in a more context-sensitive way.

Compatible Substitutions Optimization (CoSO) is motivated by the need for explicit context awareness in the original genotype space. Instead of treating a substitution as an isolated textual edit, CoSO considers whether the inserted fragment is compatible with the recipient context. This keeps the method close to the discrete representation and makes the proposed modifications interpretable as concrete genotype-level changes. However, compatibility does not by itself guarantee improvement, so candidate substitutions must still be verified by the true fitness evaluation.

Latent Space Optimization (LSO) is motivated by a complementary idea. Rather than manually defining all useful neighbourhood relations in the symbolic genotype space, an autoencoder may learn a continuous representation in which related genotypes are mapped to nearby latent vectors. If this representation captures relevant syntactic or structural regularities, continuous search methods can be applied in the latent space and the resulting candidates can be decoded back into genotypes. This approach may smooth the search space, but it also introduces risks related to reconstruction quality, validity, diversity, and the relation between latent-space movement and actual fitness improvement.

The comparison between CoSO and LSO is therefore motivated by a broader question: how should optimization methods handle context-dependent, variable-length representations in evolutionary design? CoSO represents an explicit and discrete way of using representation knowledge, while LSO represents a learned and continuous way of constructing a search space. Framsticks \fone{} and the \vertpos{} objective are used in this thesis as a concrete experimental case study for this comparison.

The main aim of this thesis is to compare two representation-aware approaches to optimization in evolutionary design: Compatible Substitutions Optimization (CoSO) and Latent Space Optimization (LSO). Both approaches address the broader difficulty of optimizing structured, variable-length, context-dependent genotypes. The comparison is not intended to prove that either approach universally replaces standard evolutionary algorithms. Instead, the thesis aims to determine how explicit context-aware substitutions and learned continuous representations behave when they are applied to the same class of structured genotypes and evaluated under a common objective.

The empirical scope of the work instantiates this general problem using Framsticks \fone{} genotypes optimized for the \vertpos{} fitness criterion. This restriction makes it possible to define a controlled case study in which all candidate solutions are ultimately evaluated by the same simulation-based criterion. Within this scope, CoSO represents the discrete and explicit direction of the study, while LSO represents the learned continuous-representation direction.

The CoSO part of the thesis covers direct genotype-level optimization through tree-based fragment handling, compatibility-based fragment matching, archive-supported substitutions, genotype repair, and true Framsticks evaluation of generated candidates. The focus is on whether representation-aware substitutions can preserve useful structural fragments while still allowing the search to improve candidate solutions.

The LSO part of the thesis covers preparing \fone{} genotype data for autoencoder training, representing genotypes with character-based or grammar-aware encodings, training selected autoencoder architectures, decoding latent vectors back into \fone{} genotypes, and applying optimization methods in the learned latent space. The autoencoder-based investigation focuses on whether the learned representation can support productive search, not only on whether it reconstructs training examples accurately.

The thesis also includes a baseline comparison with Framsticks EA. This baseline is important because it represents the native evolutionary search procedure for the considered environment and provides a practical reference point for both CoSO and LSO. The intended outcome of the study is therefore a comparative assessment of solution quality, validity, stability, and dependence on starting conditions, rather than a single claim that one method is always superior.

The research approach follows a comparison of two representation-aware optimization strategies under a common evaluation protocol. To make this comparison concrete and experimentally controlled, the thesis uses Framsticks \fone{} genotypes and the \vertpos{} objective as the shared case study. This provides a common basis for comparing methods that operate in different search spaces while preserving the same final phenotype-level evaluation.

For CoSO, the thesis considers optimization through context-aware substitutions in the original discrete genotype representation. The method extracts coherent genotype fragments, compares them using representation-level compatibility descriptors, and tests substitutions in complete candidate genotypes. This approach is analysed as an explicit attempt to use information about the compatibility of genotype fragments with their surrounding context.

For LSO, the research approach consists of learning continuous representations of \fone{} genotypes with autoencoders and then applying search operators to vectors in the latent space. A candidate latent vector is decoded into an \fone{} genotype and evaluated with the same Framsticks fitness criterion as the baseline. This makes it possible to compare latent-space search with direct genotype-level optimization while preserving a common final evaluation procedure. The implemented LSO component considers several families of autoencoder models and several continuous or population-based optimization strategies, but their technical details are introduced only in the methods and experimental chapters.

The comparison is designed to separate three related questions. First, it examines whether generated or modified genotypes remain valid and evaluable. Second, it assesses whether the search process improves the fitness of starting genotypes. Third, it compares the stability of methods across different starting conditions. This structure reflects the fact that a representation can be syntactically convenient without necessarily producing better optimization results, and that a high-performing single run is not sufficient evidence of a stable optimization method.


\section{Thesis structure}

The remainder of the thesis is organized as follows. Chapter~2 presents the literature and background required for the study. It introduces evolutionary design with variable-length genotypes, the Framsticks environment and the \fone{} representation, context-aware genotype modification, Compatible Substitutions Optimization, autoencoders, variational autoencoders, and the general idea of Latent Space Optimization.

Chapter~3 describes the methods and implemented components. It covers the dataset and fitness evaluation procedure, the grammar-based handling of \fone{} genotypes, the CoSO component, the autoencoder representations, latent-space optimization methods, the baseline evolutionary method, and the software architecture used to run the experiments.

Chapter~4 defines the experimental design. It formulates the research questions, identifies the compared methods, describes the benchmark setup and evaluation metrics, and explains the experimental protocol and reproducibility considerations. Chapter~5 reports the experimental results, including reconstruction and validity analyses for autoencoders, CoSO results, latent-space optimization results, comparisons with Framsticks EA, a direct comparison between CoSO and Latent Space Optimization, and analyses conditioned on the initial fitness range.

Chapter~6 discusses the results and concludes the thesis. It summarizes the main findings, analyses the strengths and limitations of CoSO and LSO, compares the two approaches, discusses threats to validity, and outlines possible directions for future work. Supplementary material is provided in the appendix where appropriate.
